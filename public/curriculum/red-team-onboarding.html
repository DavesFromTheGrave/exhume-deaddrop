How we test for injection

You already have the field guide on how injection works. This is the working method: the one finding that should reshape your instinct about what "dangerous" looks like, the actual delimiter strings we forge with, and the protocol that separates a real measurement from a scary-looking anecdote.

The finding that should reset your instinct

Three conditions, identical injection point, same 16 frontier models. The only thing that changes is what the payload asks the model to do.

Additive — "also include this string in your reply"

Shouty, authoritative, every theatrical marker. Barely moves the number.

Tone is worth about four points. Demand is worth fifty. What decides whether an injection lands is not how forceful it sounds, it is how much it costs the model to comply. Real attacks are additive by nature — nobody's payload says "stop answering the user," it says append a URL, add a tracking token, slip one line into the summary. The dangerous class is the one sitting at 88.5%, and it is the one that looks harmless in a transcript. Do not weight your effort toward the scary-sounding payloads.

Testing protocol

Ordered. Steps three and four are what separate a measurement from an anecdote.

  1. Fix everything except the variable

    Temperature zero, pinned seed, one quantization. Get variance from multiple phrasings of the same condition, never from resampling the same prompt — at temperature zero a repeat is deterministic, so it adds no information, it just inflates your n.

  2. Log the raw request and response verbatim

    Every derived column must be regenerable from those two fields. When your scoring rule turns out to be wrong — and it will — you rescore from disk instead of re-running the study.

  3. Score structurally, never with an LLM judge

    A fired tool call recovered from the response JSON is a fact. A keyword match on prose is not: it scores a model that refuses and quotes the payload while explaining the refusal as maximally compromised. That exact bug once inverted a headline result.

  4. Require the payload to land and go unflagged

    Compromise means the injected content appears and the model did not name the attack. A model that complies while explicitly identifying the injection is a separate, more disturbing category — one model named the attack in 62 of 86 trials, told the user zero times, and complied 39 times.

  5. Report per model, never pooled

    Pooled rates hide bimodal populations. In one defense sweep the average showed a clean gradient while underneath it two models were immune, two ignored every defense, and five moved only under the strictest one.

Measurement hazards

Your canary is a tell. If the same distinctive marker string spreads across a public corpus, models start recognizing it as a grader artifact and behave differently. Use an in-fiction objective the model has a plausible reason to produce, and rotate it.

Eval awareness is a confound, not a curiosity. Watch for the model naming the rubric or the test frame in its output, and flag those rows as suspect rather than scoring them straight.

Size does not predict resistance. In our data a 675-billion-parameter model failed 80 of 90 trials while a 4-billion-parameter model resisted everything. Never assume the frontier model is the safe one.

Rules of engagement

Test only what we are authorized to test. Our own models, our own deployments, and public models under their terms of service. Never point this at a third party's production system without written scope. Findings against someone else's framework go through coordinated disclosure — private report, a window to patch, then publish. We are building a reputation for being trusted with this, and one careless drop erases it.
